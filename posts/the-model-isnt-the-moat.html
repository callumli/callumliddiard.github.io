The model isn't the moat

For months, a specialist AI tool sat next to Copilot and people couldn't tell them apart. Then it started doing the work instead of answering questions about it.

Part of my job is getting bankers to actually use the AI tools we give them. Two of those tools are Microsoft Copilot and Rogo, an AI platform built for finance. For a long time, I couldn't tell you which one was winning, because people used them for the same thing: ask a question, get an answer, paste it somewhere else.

When two tools do the same job, the one that already lives inside Outlook and Excel wins by default. That was roughly the story with Rogo Pro, the chat version. It was good. It was on par with Copilot. Being on par wasn't enough to change anyone's habits.

What changed

Felix changed it. Felix is Rogo's agent, and it doesn't wait for a question. You hand it a multi-step piece of work, like screening a set of deals, drafting a first-pass memo, or working through a data room, and it runs the whole thing. That's a different category from chat. A chatbot saves you a search. An agent saves you an afternoon.

That's when Rogo stopped being "the other chatbot" and started being a tool people asked for by name.

Why Copilot didn't get there first

Copilot is built to work inside Microsoft 365, and it's very good there. It knows your inbox, your documents, and your meetings. But that scope is both its strength and its ceiling. It's built to understand your files. It isn't built to understand how a banker thinks about a comps table or what a client expects to see in a pitch.

A tool built for one industry has a sharper focus. It knows the vocabulary, the formats, and the audience. A memo for a managing director should read differently from a memo for a marketing team, and a specialist tool gets that right without being told.

I used to think the edge was the models

My first theory was that Rogo's advantage was access to multiple models. It can lean on GPT for some tasks and Claude for others, and take the best of each. That's a real advantage. It's also a shrinking one: Copilot now lets people choose between OpenAI and Anthropic models too.

Access to the best model is turning into table stakes. Knowing the job is the part that's hard to copy.

Model flexibility still matters, but as insurance rather than a moat. The best model changes every few months, and a tool that can swap models underneath you won't go stale. What's hard to replicate is everything built on top: the industry knowledge, the workflows, and the judgment about what a finished piece of work should look like.

What I'd tell anyone rolling out AI tools

  • Don't evaluate a tool by which model it runs. Evaluate it by which workflow it can finish end to end.
  • Being "as good as" the tool people already have isn't enough. Habits only move when the new tool does something the old one can't.
  • Ask what the tool does when nobody is typing into it. That's where the step change is.

The next phase of enterprise AI won't be won by whoever has the best model. Everyone will have the best model. It'll be won by whoever understands the work best.
